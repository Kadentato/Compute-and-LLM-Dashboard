This site runs two independent trackers. This page states, for each, what is measured, where every number comes from, how much weight each source can carry, and what the data cannot say. Jump to the compute price tracker or the LLM usage share tracker.

Compute price tracker

The compute tracker measures what it costs to rent a data-center GPU by the hour: the daily benchmark rates the announced futures contracts will settle against, the gap between those benchmarks, where they sit relative to the physical market, the price of each chip against the compute it delivers, and the published forward curve.

What it measures — and what it does not

The headline series are standardized rental rates: like-for-like prices adjusted to a common contract, region and hardware basis. They are not invoices. No individual buyer pays the index — a rate card from a single provider will differ from it, often by more than 20%, which is why the dispersion panel exists. The tracker covers neo-cloud on-demand pricing; hyperscaler list rates, reserved capacity, private negotiated deals and spot-tier capacity sit outside the index basis and are either excluded or labelled separately.

Coverage is three GPUs — H100, B200, A100 — because those are the contracts' reference assets and the ones the underlying research report analyses. B200 and A100 dollar levels are derived: the author-calculated ratio indices (.B200H100, .A100H100) applied to the H100 print, not independent published dollar series.

Data grading

Every panel on the dashboard carries one of three grades, because the sources are not of equal quality and presenting them as if they were would be misleading:

GradeWhat it isUse it for
Settlement-grade Standardized daily benchmarks published as financial products, each carrying a Bloomberg ticker — the series the announced contracts name as their reference. Marks and basis.
Indicative Public rate cards and marketplace listings, self-reported by providers, with heterogeneous contract terms. Range and direction only — never as a price.
Derived / part-digitized Computed from a fixed benchmark constant, or read off a published chart. Carries the stated model or read-off error.

Raw responses from all three daily sources are stored verbatim under data/raw/<source>/<date>.json before any processing, so every derived number can be traced back to what the source actually returned that day.

The seam between the export and the live feeds

The daily series are a Bloomberg export through 25 Aug 2026, extended from 26 Aug by the public feeds. Ratios and rebased values on extension days are computed from the public prints on the same basis as the report's custom indices. A break in a line is a missing print, not a zero — nothing is carried forward to fill a gap. Because Silicon Data and gpus.io publish no history, each missed collector run is one permanently missing daily observation for those two; Ornn backfills itself.

Cross-benchmark basis

The basis is always a same-day comparison: it is computed only on dates where both benchmarks printed, never by comparing the latest value of one against a differently-dated value of the other. Because the two sources publish on different schedules, the basis can therefore be older than the levels shown beside it, so any figure that is not from the newest available date carries its own date stamp on the dashboard.

Performance normalization

Price ratios only mean something against a productivity benchmark, and no single cross-generation multiple exists — it varies by workload, numeric precision and software vintage. Flat constants from dated public rounds are applied: B200 = 2.2x H100 (MLPerf Training v4.1, Nov 2024; 2.0x on GPT-3 175B pre-training, 2.2x on Llama 2 70B fine-tuning) and A100 = 0.45x H100 (from MLPerf Inference v3.0, Apr 2023). The A100 figure is frozen at 2023 vintage because A100 has not been submitted to a subsequent LLM round — it is the weakest constant on the site. Sensitivity: the B200 at-parity conclusion holds across 2.0–2.5x, and the A100 above-parity conclusion across 0.40–0.50x. Software gains typically accrue to newer chips after launch, so the applied constants are conservative for B200 late in the sample.

Price dispersion

Percentiles are taken across provider medians, not raw offers: one marketplace accounts for roughly 45% of all listings, so an offer-level percentile would describe that venue rather than the market. Listings marked unavailable are excluded. Reserved and serverless listings are collected but never drawn. The default high-confidence view keeps on-demand listings quoted by at least 8 providers and shows only the interquartile range, because with a handful of quotes the cheapest and dearest are single providers rather than a market; the threshold is a judgement call, not a standard. All listings restores spot, thinly-quoted rows and full ranges. Everything is drawn as a percentage of that GPU's own index so the chips are comparable and the readable quantity is basis rather than level.

Forward curve

Both curves are published by Silicon Data; neither is derived here. Silicon Data backs the forward rates out of its own term structure by no-arbitrage: a term rate is the average price of the months it covers, so committing to an N-month term must cost the same as rolling through the implied monthly forwards. The six-month values are taken exactly from the portal; intermediate tenors are digitized from its published charts and carry read-off error of a few cents. The averaging identity is verified on the published numbers — averaging each curve reproduces its own six-month term rate to within two cents. The month-by-month inversion is not reliable at this precision: recovering individual forwards from consecutive term rates multiplies each read-off error by the tenor and diverges by up to 27 cents on B200, so the monthly shape should be read as the portal's, not as something this site reconstructs.

Realized volatility

Annualized standard deviation of daily log returns over the trailing 30 prints, scaled by √365 because the index publishes every calendar day. Assessed indices are smoothed by construction, so this is a floor on traded volatility rather than an estimate of it — it should not be used directly to price optionality.

Known limitations

(1) No GPU futures contract had final regulatory approval at the data date; the CME (Silicon Data) and ICE (Ornn) initiatives remain pending, so these are the designated settlement references, not live ones. (2) Silicon Data's 5 December 2025 index revision overlaps the mid-December B200/H100 parity crossing, so the crossing date is treated as revision-affected and the sustained 2026 sub-parity level as the robust finding. (3) Performance constants are point estimates within documented ranges, and the A100 ratio is frozen at 2023 vintage. (4) Forward intermediate tenors are digitized. (5) Dispersion listings are self-reported and availability is unverified. (6) All index levels reflect standardized neo-cloud on-demand pricing; spot-tier, reserved and hyperscaler rates differ materially and are out of scope.

Pipeline

collector/collect_gpu.py (standard-library Python only) fetches all three public sources on two scattered daily schedules via .github/workflows/collect-gpu.yml, stores raw responses, derives compute/dataFiles/gpu_live.json, and commits only when new data actually arrived. Sources fail independently: one broken source does not stop the others, and the run exits non-zero so a failure is visible rather than silent.

LLM usage share tracker

The LLM tracker measures the share of tokens routed through public AI gateways that go to open-weight versus closed-frontier models, daily, with no manual data entry.

What it measures — and what it does not

Gateway traffic is developer and application traffic: agents, coding tools, SaaS products, hobby projects. Consumer chat (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude.ai, Grok) never touches a gateway, and no lab publishes model-level token counts. There is no public source for "share of all tokens in the world," and this tracker does not pretend to have one. The closest automatic consumer signal — Cloudflare Radar's DNS popularity ranking — is shown as rank order only, because that is all it is.

Classification rules

Open weight means the model's weights are downloadable under any licence, including non-commercial. Key decisions, tagged per model rather than per lab:

  • gpt-oss counts as open although OpenAI is otherwise closed; Gemma counts as open although Gemini is closed.
  • Mistral is split per model: released-weights models are open; API-only models (Large, Medium, Codestral 25xx, Ministral 3B) are closed.
  • Cohere's Command-R family is open (downloadable, CC-BY-NC); its North platform models are closed.
  • Cloaked pre-release models (openrouter/*-alpha, stealth/*) are tagged unknown, not guessed.
  • Each gateway's aggregated remainder ("other" / "Other") cannot be classified and is shown as unattributable, never allocated.

The full mapping lives in model_classification.json, versioned in the repository so every reclassification is visible in history. Models the table does not yet cover appear in the "unmapped" notice on the full tracker page — the table maintains itself by exception.

Known limitations

  • Top-N truncation. Both gateways name only their top models. On Vercel this is severe: before May 2026, open-weight usage sat inside the unnamed "Other" bucket, so the early open-share line is a floor, not a measurement.
  • Gateway selection bias. OpenRouter skews cost-sensitive and hobbyist; Vercel skews production apps. The spread between them is a finding, and the two series are never blended.
  • Coverage gaps. Text tokens only (image/video generation is excluded); no visibility into direct-to-lab API traffic (apps calling OpenAI or Anthropic without a gateway); Vercel publishes shares with no absolute volumes.
  • Headline denominator. Share figures are percentages of classifiable tokens; the composition charts show how much is excluded each day.

Pipeline

A stdlib-Python collector runs via GitHub Actions on several scattered daily schedules (GitHub's scheduler is best-effort, so redundant slots plus backfill make timing irrelevant — a run that fetches nothing new commits nothing). Every raw response is stored verbatim under data/raw/<source>/<date>.json before any processing. The collector is idempotent, backfills missing days, fails loudly rather than writing empty data, and its classification and derivation logic is covered by a CI test suite. The site is static — a dashboard homepage plus this full analysis page — and every chart offers its data as CSV.
