GPU rental pricing: market structure, generational valuation, and forward pricing

Period covered: 1 Sep 2025 – 31 Aug 2026 · spot data as of 25 Aug 2026 (Bloomberg) · forward curves as of 31 Aug 2026 (Silicon Data portal) · sources: Silicon Data, Ornn, MLCommons MLPerf — full citations in references. Prefer the short version? The dashboard has every headline number.

Executive summary

GPU rental now has real benchmarks: two independent daily price indices and, as of this year, a published forward curve [1][2][5]. That makes it possible to ask not just what a GPU-hour costs, but what it should cost. Over the twelve months to August 2026, spot rates firmed across the board — H100 up 30%, A100 up 16%, B200 up 7% from a launch-elevated base [1]. The more important story is relative: the premium for the newest chip kept shrinking, and the market has repriced Blackwell from a scarcity asset to a chip priced on its productivity.

Three findings anchor the analysis. First, the two H100 benchmarks end the year within 9% of each other but disagreed by as much as 30% along the way, and the spread between them works as a stress gauge — widest through the spring, closed by summer [1][2]. Second, the B200/H100 price ratio fell from about 2.57x to 2.11x and has held at or below the 2.2x training-performance parity line since mid-December 2025, apart from the late-March squeeze and a ten-day July episode [1][7]. The crossing date itself coincides with a documented Silicon Data index revision [10], but the sub-parity level has persisted for eight months on consistent methodology since. Per unit of compute, B200 is now the cheap chip — decisively so for inference. Third, A100 held a 0.53–0.70x ratio all year, well above its ~0.45x performance parity [8]: the oldest chip in the set trades at a premium to its productivity, priced for availability and low entry cost, and shows no sign of decaying further.

The forward market tells a consistent story. Silicon Data's six-month curves put implied forwards 7–10% below spot for all three GPUs, after a projected tightness hump around two to four months out [5]. Term rates sit at roughly the average of the forward path, so locking capacity today costs about what the market expects spot to average anyway. The value of the lock is certainty and squeeze protection, not a discount.

1 · Market and data infrastructure

Two index families benchmark this market. Silicon Data publishes daily indices built from broad observation across neocloud and hyperscaler providers, standardized for hardware spec, contract terms, and geography; they carry Bloomberg tickers (SDH100RT, SDB200RT, SDA100RT) and underpin a pending CME Group futures partnership [1][4]. Ornn publishes a transaction-weighted index (Bloomberg: ORNNH100) built from negotiated deal levels, and partners with ICE on cash-settled futures [2]. The split mirrors the assessed-index versus traded-print distinction familiar from commodity benchmarks.

This analysis uses Silicon Data as the primary source and Ornn as cross-validation, on the strength of the former's broader coverage and documented standardization. All ratios are quoted against H100 — the most liquid rental market and the natural reference asset. As of 25 August 2026: H100 $2.68, B200 $5.66, A100 $1.61 per GPU-hour (B200 and A100 dollar levels derived from author-calculated ratios applied to the SDH100RT print) [1].

The B200/H100 ratio fell from about 2.57x to 2.11x. It had already declined to 2.22x by mid-December on the original index methodology; on 5 December 2025 Silicon Data announced a major revision to its A100 and H100 indices [10], the H100 index stepped up over the following weeks, and the ratio's crossing below 2.2x coincides with that revision. The precise crossing date therefore reflects both market movement and the index change. The robust finding is what followed: on the revised methodology, applied consistently since, the ratio held 1.80–2.15x from January through August — at or below training parity for eight months — apart from the late-March squeeze (peak 2.46x) and a ten-day episode in early-to-mid July (peak 2.26x). Against inference throughput (parity near 3.5x), B200 is decisively cheap [7][9].

A100 held 0.53–0.70x all year, never approaching its ~0.45x parity line [8]: legacy capacity has found a stable floor above its productivity, priced for availability, low absolute cost, and mature software, not efficiency.

3 · Performance normalization: constants and derivation

Price ratios only mean something against a productivity benchmark, and no single cross-generation multiple exists — it varies by workload, numeric precision, and software vintage. Derivation. B200/H100 training: MLPerf Training v4.1, Nov 2024 — 2.0x on GPT-3 175B pre-training, 2.2x on Llama 2 70B fine-tuning, per GPU; 2.2x applied [7]. H100/A100: MLPerf Inference v3.0, Apr 2023 — ~2.2x training applied, inference haircut from the 4.5x best case to ~3.5x; A100 has not been submitted since 2023, so this ratio is frozen at that vintage [8]. B200 inference: midpoint of the 2.5–4x range observed vs Hopper-class across MLPerf Inference v4.1–v5.1; low-precision (FP4 vs FP8) and H200-based comparisons introduce stated caveats [9]. Sensitivity: the B200 parity conclusion holds across 2.0–2.5x; the A100 premium conclusion holds across 0.40–0.50x. Software gains typically accrue to newer chips post-launch, so the applied constants are conservative for B200 late in the sample.

4 · Forward market: term structure and implied expectations

Silicon Data publishes two curves per GPU, and the distinction matters. The term rate is what a buyer locks in today for a fixed term — an actual, transactable price. The forward rate is the market's implied expectation of spot at a future month, derived by standard no-arbitrage methods [4][5]. Term rates are contract economics; forward rates are collective expectations, with no guarantee attached.

GPU rental term structure and implied forward rates, 6-month horizon

Blue = term rate (lock in today). Amber = implied forward (expected future spot). Default view shares one scale across the three panels; the USD view uses each GPU's own scale.

Source: Silicon Data GPU Forward Curve, portal charts (Neocloud), read 31 Aug 2026 [5]. Six-month headline values exact per portal; intermediate points digitized from the charts. Spot anchors (~$2.63 H100) differ slightly from the 25 Aug Bloomberg prints ($2.68) because of the later read date.

All three forward curves share one signature: a hump at roughly two months (A100, B200) to four months (H100), then a decline to six-month forwards 7–10% below spot (H100 −9.5%, B200 −6.9%, A100 −6.8%). The market is pricing a year-end squeeze followed by relief as supply lands. H100 shows the steepest six-month decline, which inverts the 36-month ordering, where A100 is steepest at −15% and B200 shallowest at −8% [3][6]. The two horizons are consistent rather than contradictory: near term, Hopper is most exposed to arriving Blackwell supply; long term, age-driven decay dominates.

A consistency check supports the digitized data: averaging each GPU's forward rates across the seven observed tenors reproduces its six-month term rate to within two cents (H100 2.52 vs 2.52; B200 5.45 vs 5.47; A100 1.59 vs 1.60), as no-arbitrage construction requires. And with term rates at roughly the average of the forward path, commitment is currently priced at expected spot: the lock buys certainty and squeeze protection, not a discount.

5 · Implications

For buyers: current-generation hardware is the value purchase. B200 at 2.11x H100 is at or below training-compute parity and well below inference parity, while A100's low sticker hides the highest cost per unit of work in the set. Buyers with known six-month needs face a term structure that prices certainty at about expected spot — and the forward hump argues for locking ahead of the projected fourth-quarter tightness, not after it.

For analysts and lenders: the compression of the Blackwell premium alongside age-ordered backwardation at long tenors is what a commoditizing market looks like. New silicon enters as a scarcity asset and converges to productivity-based pricing as software matures and supply arrives — a cycle B200 completed within roughly a year of volume availability. And A100's stable above-parity floor shows that legacy capacity migrates down-market to workloads where its pricing still clears. In this market, obsolescence is a repricing, not a retirement.

Appendix: methodology and limitations

Data

Spot: Silicon Data daily indices via Bloomberg (SDH100RT, SDB200RT, SDA100RT), standardized neocloud on-demand basis, and Ornn ORNNH100, 1 Sep 2025 – 25 Aug 2026. B200 and A100 dollar levels are author-calculated ratios (.B200H100, .A100H100 custom indices) times the SDH100RT print. Forward: Silicon Data portal charts, Neocloud basis, read 31 Aug 2026; six-month headline values exact, intermediate points digitized. Performance: MLPerf rounds as cited. This page: the daily chart series are the actual index prints from a Bloomberg Terminal export (retrieved 25 Aug 2026), including the custom ratio indices (.B200H100, .A100H100); missing prints appear as breaks in a line rather than being carried forward. From 26 Aug 2026 the daily series extend automatically: a collector fetches the latest prints once a day from the sources’ own public feeds — Silicon Data’s indices page (SDH100RT, SDA100RT, SDB200RT and MI300X, one print per business day, recorded as displayed) and Ornn’s public index API (settled daily OCPI values, which also filled the 20–25 Aug prints missing from the terminal export). Ratios and rebased values on extension days are computed from those prints on the same basis as the report’s custom indices; raw responses are archived in the repository before processing.

Limitations

(1) Both series have occasional missing daily prints in the export (Ornn: 31 May, 19 Jun, 29 Jul, and 20–25 Aug 2026; Silicon Data H100: eight scattered days including 11 Apr and 1–2 May); the 20–25 Aug Ornn prints absent from the export have since been filled from Ornn’s public feed. (2) Silicon Data's 5 December 2025 index revision [10] overlaps the mid-December H100 step-up and the B200/H100 parity crossing; the crossing date is treated as revision-affected and the sustained 2026 sub-parity level as the robust finding. No corresponding Ornn revision announcement was found; the ORNNH100 spike of late December (1.84 to 2.21, fully reverted by early January) is consistent with a transient market event or thin-print volatility in a then-three-month-old transaction index, though third-party analysis notes undated methodology evolution between Ornn's October 2025 documentation and its 2026 product description. (3) Performance constants are point estimates within documented ranges; the A100/H100 ratio is frozen at April 2023 vintage. (4) Forward intermediate tenors are digitized from charts; exact values via Silicon Data's API would supersede them. (5) All levels reflect standardized neocloud on-demand pricing; spot-tier, reserved, and hyperscaler rates differ materially and are out of scope. (6) No GPU futures contract had final regulatory approval at the data date; the CME (Silicon Data) and ICE (Ornn) initiatives remain pending [11].
