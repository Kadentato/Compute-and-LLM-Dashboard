Executive summary

GPU rental now has real benchmarks: two independent daily price indices and, as of this year, a published forward curve [1][2][5]. That makes it possible to ask not just what a GPU-hour costs, but what it should cost. Over the twelve months to August 2026, spot rates firmed across the board — H100 up 30%, A100 up 16%, B200 up 7% from a launch-elevated base [1]. The more important story is relative: the premium for the newest chip kept shrinking, and the market has repriced Blackwell from a scarcity asset to a chip priced on its productivity.

Three findings anchor the analysis. First, the two H100 benchmarks end the year within 9% of each other but disagreed by as much as 30% along the way, and the spread between them works as a stress gauge — widest through the spring, closed by summer [1][2]. Second, the B200/H100 price ratio fell from about 2.57x to 2.11x and has held at or below the 2.2x training-performance parity line since mid-December 2025, apart from the late-March squeeze and a ten-day July episode [1][7]. The crossing date itself coincides with a documented Silicon Data index revision [10], but the sub-parity level has persisted for eight months on consistent methodology since. Per unit of compute, B200 is now the cheap chip — decisively so for inference. Third, A100 held a 0.53–0.70x ratio all year, well above its ~0.45x performance parity [8]: the oldest chip in the set trades at a premium to its productivity, priced for availability and low entry cost, and shows no sign of decaying further.

The forward market tells a consistent story. Silicon Data's six-month curves put implied forwards 7–10% below spot for all three GPUs, after a projected tightness hump around two to four months out [5]. Term rates sit at roughly the average of the forward path, so locking capacity today costs about what the market expects spot to average anyway. The value of the lock is certainty and squeeze protection, not a discount.

1 · Market and data infrastructure

Two index families benchmark this market. Silicon Data publishes daily indices built from broad observation across neocloud and hyperscaler providers, standardized for hardware spec, contract terms, and geography; they carry Bloomberg tickers (SDH100RT, SDB200RT, SDA100RT) and underpin a pending CME Group futures partnership [1][4]. Ornn publishes a transaction-weighted index (Bloomberg: ORNNH100) built from negotiated deal levels, and partners with ICE on cash-settled futures [2]. The split mirrors the assessed-index versus traded-print distinction familiar from commodity benchmarks.

This analysis uses Silicon Data as the primary source and Ornn as cross-validation, on the strength of the former's broader coverage and documented standardization. All ratios are quoted against H100 — the most liquid rental market and the natural reference asset. As of 25 August 2026: H100 $2.68, B200 $5.66, A100 $1.61 per GPU-hour (B200 and A100 dollar levels derived from author-calculated ratios applied to the SDH100RT print) [1].

The B200/H100 ratio fell from about 2.57x to 2.11x. It had already declined to 2.22x by mid-December on the original index methodology; on 5 December 2025 Silicon Data announced a major revision to its A100 and H100 indices [10], the H100 index stepped up over the following weeks, and the ratio's crossing below 2.2x coincides with that revision. The precise crossing date therefore reflects both market movement and the index change. The robust finding is what followed: on the revised methodology, applied consistently since, the ratio held 1.80–2.15x from January through August — at or below training parity for eight months — apart from the late-March squeeze (peak 2.46x) and a ten-day episode in early-to-mid July (peak 2.26x). Against inference throughput (parity near 3.5x), B200 is decisively cheap [7][9].

A100 held 0.53–0.70x all year, never approaching its ~0.45x parity line [8]: legacy capacity has found a stable floor above its productivity, priced for availability, low absolute cost, and mature software, not efficiency.

2b · Basis risk: the index versus the physical market

An index is only useful as a settlement reference if it sits credibly inside the market it represents. That check matters here more than in most commodities, because a GPU-hour is not a standardized delivery: the same chip is sold on-demand, spot, and under multi-month commitment, in different regions, with different interconnect and support attached. The listed price therefore varies severalfold across venues for identical silicon.

Source: gpus.io public catalogue, collected daily; neo-cloud and marketplace providers only, matching the neo-cloud basis of the index. Listings marked unavailable are excluded; on-demand and spot are drawn, while reserved and serverless listings are collected but not shown. Indicative, not settlement-grade — these are rate cards with heterogeneous contract terms, so the range is wide by construction and no individual listing is comparable to the index.

Two things follow for anyone pricing exposure to this market. First, the assessed index sits below the median listed on-demand rate, which is what a basis-adjusted, like-for-like methodology should produce: it strips the premium attached to short-commitment, fully-supported capacity. A hedge struck against the index will not track a spot invoice from a single provider, and the gap is structural rather than noise. Second, the spot tier clears far under the index, so interruptible capacity is not a substitute for hedged firm capacity when comparing costs.

The honest limitation: this panel is the weakest data on the site. Listings are self-reported by providers, availability is not verified, one marketplace accounts for roughly 45% of all listings (which is why percentiles are taken across provider medians rather than offers), and the mix of rental types shifts as providers reprice. It is included because ignoring the physical market would leave the index looking more authoritative than it is — but it should be read as a range and a direction, never as a price.

The collapse everyone quotes is mostly a tier comparison. The familiar line — H100s went from $8 an hour to under $3 — takes a 2023 hyperscaler on-demand rate and sets it against a 2025 marketplace rate. Those are different products: different commitment terms, different support, different availability guarantees. Silicon Data's own summary of the period puts it plainly, describing 2024 as "Hyperscaler ~$9+, Marketplace/Neocloud ~$2.5–$3.0" and late 2025 as "Marketplace clusters near ~$2, Neocloud near ~$3.3, Hyperscaler near ~$6.3." Read down a single tier instead of across tiers and the decline is far smaller — and on the neocloud line it is not a decline at all: ~$2.99 median in H2 2024 against ~$3.3 a year later.

Why these are not plotted with the index. Even the neocloud figures above come from a different construction than the daily index this page tracks: for Sep 2025 the blog's neocloud tier sits near $3.3 while the index reads about $2.06. That gap is roughly 60% — larger than most of the price moves anyone would want to read off a chart — so splicing them would manufacture a trend out of a definitional change. The honest use of this table is as an order-of-magnitude anchor for where the market sat before the tracked window, not as the left-hand end of the series.

What survives that caution is still useful. The 2023 scarcity was real and extreme, and the easing through 2024 was real. But the easing was concentrated in the emergence of a cheaper tier — marketplaces and neoclouds reselling capacity — rather than in the hyperscaler price falling much. And on the one basis this project measures continuously, the direction since Sep 2025 has been up: the constant-quality index has risen about 25% in twelve months. Any argument that the shortage resolved between 2023 and today has to explain why the like-for-like series turned back up through a generation launch.

3 · Performance normalization: constants and derivation

Price ratios only mean something against a productivity benchmark, and no single cross-generation multiple exists — it varies by workload, numeric precision, and software vintage. This analysis applies flat, per-workload constants taken from dated public benchmark rounds, with the sensitivity stated.

Derivation. B200/H100 training: MLPerf Training v4.1, Nov 2024 — 2.0x on GPT-3 175B pre-training, 2.2x on Llama 2 70B fine-tuning, per GPU; 2.2x applied [7]. H100/A100: MLPerf Inference v3.0, Apr 2023 — ~2.2x training applied, inference haircut from the 4.5x best case to ~3.5x; A100 has not been submitted since 2023, so this ratio is frozen at that vintage [8]. B200 inference: midpoint of the 2.5–4x range observed vs Hopper-class across MLPerf Inference v4.1–v5.1; low-precision (FP4 vs FP8) and H200-based comparisons introduce stated caveats [9]. Sensitivity: the B200 parity conclusion holds across 2.0–2.5x; the A100 premium conclusion holds across 0.40–0.50x. Software gains typically accrue to newer chips post-launch, so the applied constants are conservative for B200 late in the sample.

The constant-quality index. Dividing each chip's rate by its performance multiple gives the cost of one H100-equivalent hour on that chip; taking the cheapest of them each day gives a constant-quality series — what it costs to buy a unit of work if you are willing to rent whichever silicon is best value. This is the series a scarcity argument needs, because a generational launch should drag it down even while any single chip's price holds. Over the twelve months to 3 Sep 2026 it rose roughly 25%, and B200 displaced H100 as the cheapest source in December 2025. Quality-adjusted compute got more expensive across a generation transition, which is the opposite of what capacity catching up with demand would produce.

Two limits on how hard that can be pushed. The constants are fixed, so the index moves only with price: it does not capture software and kernel efficiency gains on the same silicon, which lower the real cost of work and would bias the series upward relative to true cost-to-compute. And it is built on the assessed index, so it inherits that basis — the physical market's dispersion sits above it.

4 · Forward market: term structure and implied expectations

Silicon Data publishes two curves per GPU, and the distinction matters. The term rate is what a buyer locks in today for a fixed term — an actual, transactable price. The forward rate is the market's implied expectation of spot at a future month. Term rates are contract economics; forward rates are collective expectations, with no guarantee attached.

How the forward rate is obtained. Both curves are published by Silicon Data; neither is derived here. Silicon Data backs the forwards out of its own term structure by no-arbitrage [4][5]: a term rate is the average price of the months it covers, so committing to an N-month term must cost the same as rolling through the implied monthly forwards over those same N months — otherwise the two routes could be traded against each other. Inverting that relation month by month recovers the marginal month implied by consecutive term rates. What this page contributes is the collection and the check, not the derivation: every tenor is pulled exactly from the portal each day, and the identity is verified below.

What the backwardation does and does not tell you. All three curves decline, and the decline is close to monotonic in the age of the part: at 36 months (curve as of 3 Sep 2026) A100 sits −24.9% below spot, H100 −12.3%, and B200 −0.5%. It is tempting to read that as the market pricing an easing of the compute shortage. It is not — or at least not only. An H100 in 2029 is a six-year-old part competing against two later generations, and its rental rate should fall from obsolescence alone even if compute stays scarce. Note the direction of the pattern: if this curve were pricing a supply glut, the newest chip — the one still current when new capacity lands — should show the steepest expected decline. Instead it is the flattest, and the oldest part falls hardest. That shape is what depreciation looks like, not a view on the 2028 supply balance.

The consequence for anyone using this page to argue about scarcity: the forward curve of any single part cannot settle it. A constant-quality series is needed — price per unit of delivered compute, built in section 3 — and the testable proposition is whether that series falls faster than the historical rate of performance-per-dollar improvement. Spot levels are weaker still: today's price reflects the installed base against today's demand, both largely determined by capex committed one to two years ago, so a high H100 rate is evidence about past investment rather than about future supply.

Term and forward rates are collected exactly from the Silicon Data portal (every tenor, four decimals), refreshed daily. All three forward curves share one signature: a hump at roughly two months (A100, B200) to four months (H100), then a decline to six-month forwards 7–10% below spot (H100 −9.5%, B200 −6.9%, A100 −6.8%). The market is pricing a year-end squeeze followed by relief as supply lands. H100 shows the steepest six-month decline, which inverts the 36-month ordering, where A100 is steepest at −15% and B200 shallowest at −8% [3][6]. The two horizons are consistent rather than contradictory: near term, Hopper is most exposed to arriving Blackwell supply; long term, age-driven decay dominates.

A consistency check supports the derivation: averaging each GPU's forward rates across the seven observed tenors reproduces its six-month term rate to within two cents (H100 2.52 vs 2.52; B200 5.45 vs 5.47; A100 1.59 vs 1.60), which is exactly what the averaging identity requires. The month-by-month inversion remains the portal's own: recovering individual forwards from consecutive term rates is numerically unstable, so the monthly shape is taken as published rather than reconstructed here. And with term rates at roughly the average of the forward path, commitment is currently priced at expected spot: the lock buys certainty and squeeze protection, not a discount.

5 · Implications

For buyers: current-generation hardware is the value purchase. B200 at 2.11x H100 is at or below training-compute parity and well below inference parity, while A100's low sticker hides the highest cost per unit of work in the set. Buyers with known six-month needs face a term structure that prices certainty at about expected spot — and the forward hump argues for locking ahead of the projected fourth-quarter tightness, not after it.

For analysts and lenders: the compression of the Blackwell premium alongside age-ordered backwardation at long tenors is what a commoditizing market looks like. New silicon enters as a scarcity asset and converges to productivity-based pricing as software matures and supply arrives — a cycle B200 completed within roughly a year of volume availability. And A100's stable above-parity floor shows that legacy capacity migrates down-market to workloads where its pricing still clears. In this market, obsolescence is a repricing, not a retirement.

Appendix: methodology and limitations

Data

Spot: Silicon Data daily indices via Bloomberg (SDH100RT, SDB200RT, SDA100RT), standardized neocloud on-demand basis, and Ornn ORNNH100, 1 Sep 2025 – 25 Aug 2026. B200 and A100 dollar levels are author-calculated ratios (.B200H100, .A100H100 custom indices) times the SDH100RT print. Forward: Silicon Data portal, Neocloud basis, collected daily — exact term and forward rates at every tenor out to 36 months. Performance: MLPerf rounds as cited. This page: the daily chart series are the actual index prints from a Bloomberg Terminal export (retrieved 25 Aug 2026), including the custom ratio indices (.B200H100, .A100H100); missing prints appear as breaks in a line rather than being carried forward. From 26 Aug 2026 the daily series extend automatically: a collector fetches the latest prints once a day from the sources’ own public feeds — Silicon Data’s indices page (SDH100RT, SDA100RT, SDB200RT and MI300X, one print per business day, recorded as displayed) and Ornn’s public index API (settled daily OCPI values, which also filled the 20–25 Aug prints missing from the terminal export). Ratios and rebased values on extension days are computed from those prints on the same basis as the report’s custom indices; raw responses are archived in the repository before processing.

Limitations

(1) Both series have occasional missing daily prints in the export (Ornn: 31 May, 19 Jun, 29 Jul, and 20–25 Aug 2026; Silicon Data H100: eight scattered days including 11 Apr and 1–2 May); the 20–25 Aug Ornn prints absent from the export have since been filled from Ornn’s public feed. (2) Silicon Data's 5 December 2025 index revision [10] overlaps the mid-December H100 step-up and the B200/H100 parity crossing; the crossing date is treated as revision-affected and the sustained 2026 sub-parity level as the robust finding. No corresponding Ornn revision announcement was found; the ORNNH100 spike of late December (1.84 to 2.21, fully reverted by early January) is consistent with a transient market event or thin-print volatility in a then-three-month-old transaction index, though third-party analysis notes undated methodology evolution between Ornn's October 2025 documentation and its 2026 product description. (3) Performance constants are point estimates within documented ranges; the A100/H100 ratio is frozen at April 2023 vintage. (4) Forward rates are now collected exactly from Silicon Data's public portal, superseding the earlier chart read-offs. (5) All levels reflect standardized neocloud on-demand pricing; spot-tier, reserved, and hyperscaler rates differ materially and are out of scope. (6) No GPU futures contract had final regulatory approval at the data date; the CME (Silicon Data) and ICE (Ornn) initiatives remain pending [11].
